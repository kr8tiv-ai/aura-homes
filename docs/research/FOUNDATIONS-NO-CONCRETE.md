# Foundations Without Concrete — Alberta Reality Check

*Research node output, Aug 9, 2026. Mandate under test: NO conventional concrete in Aura homes wherever possible — driven/screw piles "protected from any leakage into the ground"; hempcrete (hemp-lime) where a concrete-like material is truly needed; nothing may affect the water table. Sources cross-checked per the honesty anchors in [GRAPH-ENGINEERING.md](../GRAPH-ENGINEERING.md); negative findings stated as plainly as positive ones.*

## Verdict

**The mandate is buildable in Alberta today, and it is cost-neutral to cost-NEGATIVE — because our spec was already most of the way there.** Galvanized steel screw piles are the accepted foundation for environmentally sensitive sites in Canada (wetland boardwalks, shoreline structures) precisely because they install with no excavation, no spoils, no wet cement chemistry in the ground, and are fully removable. They are also $19–30K *cheaper* than a concrete foundation for our 800 sqft reference home. The poly-tank preference for cistern and septic costs roughly nothing ($0 to ~+$650 on the tank). Three honesty caveats that must survive into marketing copy:

1. **"Zero leaching" is not a claim we can make.** Hot-dip galvanized piles sacrifice their zinc coating into the immediately surrounding soil over decades — that is how galvanizing works. The flux is small (micrometres of coating per year; order of a kilogram of zinc per pile released over a 30–75-year service life), zinc binds strongly to soil within centimetres of the shaft, and zinc is a micronutrient rather than a persistent toxin like arsenic — but it is not zero. The defensible claim: *no cement chemistry in the ground, no cure-water pH plume, reversible foundation, roughly an order of magnitude less embodied carbon than a concrete pier system.* Where piles sit near a high water table, spec AC228 polymer-coated piles, which encapsulate the zinc entirely.
2. **Hempcrete is not a concrete substitute below grade — for anything.** It is a non-structural insulating wall infill (compressive strength ~0.3–1 MPa; even Alberta's own block maker says the hemp blocks cannot be used below ground level). It can never be a foundation, footing, grade beam, or structural slab. The mandate's "use hempcrete where concrete-like material is needed" clause therefore applies only above grade — and in our SIP-based spec, almost nothing above grade needs a concrete-like material anyway.
3. **Grouted helical variants are concrete in disguise.** The Helical Pulldown Micropile and screw-displacement piles (Chance Drivecast class) build a portland-cement grout column around the shaft. They exist for very weak soils. They are prohibited under this mandate and the AI design rules must flag them.

## Piles: environmental truth

**What galvanized steel actually sheds.** Hot-dip galvanizing is sacrificial: the zinc layer corrodes instead of the steel. In soil, published disintegration rates run from under 5 µm/year in mild soils to 25 µm/year in hostile (acidic, saline, wet) soils; the 20 least corrosive soils in long-term testing averaged ~2 µm/year. Industry guidance for screw piles wanting a 30-year design life specifies ≥85 µm average coating. Back-of-envelope inventory (clearly an estimate): a typical residential screw pile (2 7/8"–3 1/2" shaft, ~10 ft, plus helix) carries roughly 0.5–1 kg of zinc total, released gradually into a centimetre-scale soil halo over the coating's life. Zinc adsorbs strongly to soil particles (documented soil-zinc elevation around galvanized structures is localized, not plume-forming), and central Alberta's glacial till is predominantly neutral-to-alkaline — the low-corrosion, low-mobility end of the range. The genuine risk case is acidic, sandy, high-water-table soil, where both corrosion rate and zinc mobility rise.

**Is that a water-table problem?** Canada regulates zinc in freshwater via the CCME long-term guideline, which is a hardness/pH/DOC-dependent equation (harder, higher-pH water — i.e., typical Alberta groundwater — tolerates more zinc before aquatic-life effects). The consensus position visible across corrosion literature and environmental-construction practice: buried galvanized structures are not treated as groundwater contamination sources in normal soils; guidance flags extra protection where members cross the soil/groundwater interface. Helical piles are routinely *specified into* wetlands and protected ecosystems by Canadian boardwalk and eco-tourism builders because rotation-installed piles produce no excavation, no spoils, minimal vibration, and full reversibility. That is a strong environmental record — as minimal-disturbance, not as zero-emission.

**Coating options, honestly ranked for the mandate:**
- **Hot-dip galvanized (baseline, CSA G164 / ASTM A123 class):** the industry standard; sacrificial zinc as described. Fine for normal Alberta till away from the water table.
- **AC228 polymer (EAA) coating over the steel, ≥18 mil:** one of only two corrosion-protection systems accepted alongside zinc in ICC AC358 (the helical-pile acceptance criteria); marketed with ~50-year technical life (Ram Jack class thermoplastic). Encapsulates the metal — this is the "protected from any leakage" spec the founder asked for. Premium is quote-based (not published); expect a modest per-pile upcharge. **Aura standard where piles sit near a seasonal high water table, wells, or watercourses.**
- **Fusion-bonded / high-build epoxy:** used in marine and aggressive-soil work; helix leading edges can scrape any coating during installation — a known limitation of all coated piles; the scraped zone re-passivates on galvanized piles (zinc's advantage) but stays bare on epoxy-only. Galvanized + polymer over-coat beats epoxy-only for this reason.
- **Bare (black) steel driven or screw piles:** sheds only iron oxide — environmentally benign rust — at the cost of sacrificial-thickness design (thicker wall, engineered corrosion allowance). Common in Edmonton commercial work. A legitimate zero-zinc option immediately beside a drinking well, at some capacity/lifespan penalty. Worth keeping in the AI's option set.

**Versus concrete piers — the comparison the mandate is really about.** Cast-in-place concrete cures in the ground: fresh cement pore water is caustic (pH ~12–13), fresh-concrete leachate measurably raises pH of receiving water and harms aquatic life (a documented water-quality concern, e.g. Washington State Ecology guidance), trace hexavalent chromium rides along, cast-in-place piles are explicitly problematic where groundwater flows (groundwater mingling with wet concrete produces defective pile bottoms), and hydration heat disturbs frozen ground. Concrete piers also crack in Edmonton's freeze-thaw and frost-jack when poorly placed, are permanent (removal = excavation), and carry roughly an order of magnitude more embodied CO2 than a steel pile of equal service. Precast components (tanks, piers) arrive already cured, so their in-ground leachate is far smaller than cast-in-place — the poly-tank preference below is mostly mandate consistency, weight, and corrosion immunity, not a large groundwater win; we say so honestly.

**Driven timber piles — rejected.** Untreated timber piles only last where permanently submerged below the water table (traditional practice, wrong for our pile-head-above-grade use). Pressure-treated timber (CCA class) leaches arsenic, chromium, and copper — soil under CCA structures shows arsenic at ~19x background in published studies. Under a water-table-first mandate, treated timber in the ground is strictly worse than galvanized steel. Same logic demotes preserved-wood (PWF) grade beams and treated-post foundations: modern ACQ/CA treatments still leach copper. Where a grade beam is needed across pile heads, use steel (W-section or HSS) or engineered wood kept above grade on pile caps.

## Hempcrete in Alberta: what it is and isn't

**What it is:** hemp hurd (the woody stalk core) mixed with a lime-based binder and water, cast or blocked into a wall as *insulating infill around a structural frame*. Excellent vapour-open insulation (~R-2 to R-2.5/inch), non-combustible in fire tests, carbon-storing (published LCAs show net-negative cradle-to-gate CO2e for lime-bound, moderate-density mixes).

**What it isn't:** structural, or a below-grade material. Compressive strength of cast hemp-lime is ~0.3–1 MPa — two orders of magnitude below concrete. It cannot be a foundation, footing, grade beam, slab, or pile cap. The one "structural" Alberta product — Just BioFiber's interlocking block (Airdrie) — gets its load path from a composite frame moulded around the hempcrete, and even JBF states the hemp blocks cannot be used below ground level (they make separate foam blocks for that). Anyone selling hempcrete as a foundation material is selling something that does not exist. State this plainly in the app.

**The lime question (the honest chemistry).** Hemp-lime binder is hydrated lime (calcium hydroxide) ± natural hydraulic lime or pozzolans. Lime is NOT portland cement: limestone calcined at ~900°C (vs ~1450°C clinker), no clinker phases, and it re-absorbs a large fraction of its process CO2 from the air as it carbonates back to limestone over the wall's life (published estimates: 18.5–38.4% of binder-production emissions re-sequestered in situ, on top of the hemp's biogenic storage). Two caveats: (1) fresh lime is still caustic (pH ~12.4 wet) — but hempcrete lives above grade inside a wall assembly and never touches soil or groundwater, so there is no water-table pathway; (2) **some commercial hempcrete binders blend in portland cement to speed set** — high-density cement-blended mixes can erase the carbon benefit entirely. Aura spec: cement-free lime binder only. Under the founder's rule as written ("no conventional concrete... nothing may affect the water table"), lime-bound hempcrete above grade passes; a cement-blended binder does not.

**Suppliers (Alberta-first):**
- **Just BioFiber** — Airdrie AB. Patented structural-frame hemp blocks, ~$30–35/sqft of wall, roughly at parity with ICF; 2-hour fire rating; producing since 2015.
- **Hemp Homes Canada** — Trochu AB. Precast hempcrete blocks and hemp panels from Alberta-grown hemp, with installation service. Quote-based.
- **8th Fire Innovations** — founder was lead consultant/contractor on Alberta's first code-inspected commercial hempcrete building — the permit precedent exists in this province.
- **HempWorks Canada** — Kelowna BC. Construction-grade hurd, binders, hemp batts; equipment rental and consulting (the cast-in-place supply line).
- **HempBLOCK Canada / International** — imported block system, Canadian storefront.

**Code path:** there is no CCMC listing for hempcrete (nothing hempcrete-specific in the national code). The route is an NBC/ABC **alternative solution** under Part 9, with a designer/P.Eng demonstrating equivalent performance — done before in Alberta (above), so it is a known, but paid and slower, path. Contrast with our SIP shell, which rides a CCMC listing (Insulspan 13016-R) straight through Part 9. **Consequence for the pilot: hempcrete walls would replace the SIP shell and trade our painless permit path for an alternative-solutions filing. Hempcrete is therefore a roadmap wall option, not the pilot spec.** This is not a knock on hempcrete; it is sequencing.

**The three specific "could hempcrete do X" questions:**
- **Wood-stove hearth pads — no.** CSA B365 floor protection must be durable and noncombustible: sheet metal ≥0.38 mm, grouted tile, or mortared brick. Hempcrete contains organic aggregate and is not a listed noncombustible floor-protection material. The concrete-free hearth is easy anyway: a manufactured steel hearth pad, or brick/stone laid in **lime mortar** (no portland). Note honestly: the common cement-board underlay contains portland cement — a steel pad or mortared brick avoids it entirely.
- **Grade beams — never.** 0.3–1 MPa. Physics says no. Steel or engineered-wood members on pile caps do this job.
- **Floor toppings — technically yes, practically N/A.** Non-structural hemp-lime insulating screeds exist in European practice, but our homes have wood-framed floors on piles — there is no slab to top. Nothing to replace.

## Cost impact

**Versus a conventional concrete-foundation build:** the no-concrete stack SAVES $19–30K on the foundation alone. The poly tank preference is noise ($0 to +$650); everything else in the audit was already concrete-free. The mandate makes the reference home cheaper, not dearer.

**Versus our current cost model:** ~$0. The model already carried screw piles at $6–15K and tank/cistern lines wide enough to hold the poly option. **No amounts in `data/alberta/cost-model.json` change from this research — basis fields are enriched only, so the computed totals ($199K/$301K/$444K ex-land) stand and no recompute is triggered under the totalsRule.**

**What the mandate genuinely buys (the honest pitch):** no wet cement chemistry in the ground; no cure-water pH plume near the water table; a fully reversible foundation (unscrew and the site returns to grade); winter installability; ~an order of magnitude less foundation embodied carbon; and a supplier story that is 100% Alberta. What it does not buy: literal zero leaching (galvanized zinc, quantified above) — which is why coated piles are the spec near water, and why our copy says "minimal-disturbance, reversible, cement-free" rather than "zero-impact."
